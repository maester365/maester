# Making the case for Maester

Maester is free, but rolling it out still needs someone's time, a place to run it and, often, approval. This page
helps you get that approval. It has three parts:

1. [What organisations miss without continuous monitoring](#what-organisations-miss-without-continuous-monitoring):
   real configuration failures, each with the Maester test that catches it. Use them as examples in any of the
   templates.
2. [Templates](#templates) for three audiences: general executives, a CISO, and an IT Security Adviser (ITSA). Copy
   one, replace the text in `[brackets]`, and delete what does not apply.
3. [Common questions](#common-questions) that come up in these conversations, with honest answers.

:::tip[Run Maester first]
The strongest argument is your own tenant. Run Maester once (see [Getting started](getting-started.mdx)) and quote
your own numbers: how many Critical and High failures there are, and two or three examples from the list below that
you actually found.
:::

## What organisations miss without continuous monitoring

A Microsoft 365 tenant changes every week: people join and leave, admins grant exceptions, projects add apps, and
Microsoft releases new features with new settings. Most security weaknesses in a tenant are not the result of an
attack. They are a setting that was right once, and was then changed, or quietly stopped working, without anyone
noticing. A yearly audit or a one-off assessment sees the tenant on one day. Continuous monitoring sees the day it
changed.

| What goes wrong | Why nobody notices | What it can lead to | Maester checks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A Conditional Access policy stops protecting anyone.** The group it targets is deleted, or a user or role it names no longer exists. | The policy still shows as *On* in the portal. Nothing alerts. | Users who should need MFA, or a compliant device, sign in without either. | [MT.1038](/docs/tests/MT.1038), [MT.1066](/docs/tests/MT.1066) |
| **Admins lock themselves out.** An emergency access (break glass) account is caught by a new policy, or blocked. | The account is used only in an emergency, so the problem is found during the emergency. | No way into the tenant during an outage or an incident. | [MT.1005](/docs/tests/MT.1005), [MT.1034](/docs/tests/MT.1034) |
| **Too many people hold the keys.** Global Administrators accumulate; people who left or changed role keep privileged roles. | Role assignments are rarely reviewed, and each one was justified when it was made. | A single phished account can take over the whole tenant. | [MT.1032](/docs/tests/MT.1032), [MT.1029](/docs/tests/MT.1029), [CIS.M365.1.1.3](/docs/tests/CIS.M365.1.1.3) |
| **MFA is bypassed through legacy authentication.** An exception is added for an old device or app and never removed. | MFA appears to be enforced for everyone. | Password spraying succeeds against accounts that "have MFA". | [MT.1009](/docs/tests/MT.1009), [CISA.MS.AAD.1.1](/docs/tests/CISA.MS.AAD.1.1) |
| **Users hand attackers access through an app.** Users can consent to third-party apps that read mail and files. | Consent looks like a normal sign-in prompt to the user. | Persistent access to mailboxes and files that survives a password reset. | [CIS.M365.5.1.5.1](/docs/tests/CIS.M365.5.1.5.1), [EIDSCA.AP08](/docs/tests/EIDSCA.AP08) |
| **Mail is forwarded outside the organisation.** Automatic forwarding to external domains is allowed. | Forwarding rules are set per mailbox, often by an attacker after a compromise. | Silent, continuing data loss. | [CISA.MS.EXO.1.1](/docs/tests/CISA.MS.EXO.1.1) |
| **There is no record when you need it.** Audit logging or mailbox auditing is off. | Logs are only looked at after an incident. | You cannot tell what an attacker did, or meet breach-notification and legal obligations. | [CIS.M365.3.1.1](/docs/tests/CIS.M365.3.1.1), [CISA.MS.EXO.13.1](/docs/tests/CISA.MS.EXO.13.1) |
| **Anyone can send email as you.** DMARC is missing or not enforced on a domain. | Mail still flows normally. | Invoice fraud and phishing that appears to come from your own domain. | [MT.1182](/docs/tests/MT.1182) |
| **Applications hold powerful, long-lived secrets.** A service principal with a client secret holds a permanent privileged role. | Apps are created by projects and rarely reviewed by security. | A leaked secret gives an attacker admin rights without any user sign-in or MFA. | [MT.1027](/docs/tests/MT.1027), [MT.1057](/docs/tests/MT.1057) |
| **An integration stops working.** An app registration's certificate or secret expires. | The expiry date is in the portal, but nobody is watching it. | An outage of a business process that depends on the app. | [MT.1199](/docs/tests/MT.1199) (long-running: add `-IncludeLongRunning`) |
| **Files are shared with anyone.** External sharing is opened up for one project and left open. | Sharing settings are changed in a different admin centre from the rest of security. | Sensitive documents reachable by people outside the organisation. | [CIS.M365.7.2.3](/docs/tests/CIS.M365.7.2.3) |
| **A new phishing technique works.** Device code sign-in is allowed for everyone. | It is on by default and rarely needed. | Phishing that captures a signed-in session, including MFA. | [CIS.M365.5.2.2.12](/docs/tests/CIS.M365.5.2.2.12) |

Beyond individual weaknesses, an organisation without continuous monitoring also misses:

- **Early warning.** A weakening change is found within a day, by your team, instead of months later by an auditor,
  a penetration tester or an attacker.
- **Evidence on demand.** Every run is a dated, repeatable record of which controls passed. Audit and assurance
  evidence no longer has to be gathered by hand.
- **Knowledge that stays when people leave.** Your intent ("this group must always be in this policy") is written
  down as a test, not kept in one admin's head.
- **A measurable trend.** You can report the number of open Critical and High findings each month, and show it
  going down.
- **Safe change.** Admins can make changes with the confidence that a mistake will be caught by the next run.

**From:** [Your name, role]
**Subject:** Continuous checks on our Microsoft 365 security settings

**Summary**

Our email, files, Teams and user sign-ins run on Microsoft 365. Its security depends on hundreds of settings, and
those settings change every week as people join and leave and as we add new services. Today we check them
[once a year during the audit / when something goes wrong / not at all]. I propose that we check them automatically
every day with Maester, a free tool used by [organisations like ours], so that we find a weakening change within a day
instead of at the next audit or after an incident.

**Why this matters**

Most security weaknesses in cloud services are not caused by sophisticated attacks. They are caused by a setting
that was changed, or stopped working, without anyone noticing. For example:

- [A sign-in protection that appears to be on but no longer covers anyone, because the group it applied to was deleted.]
- [Our own example from the first run: "We found [number] accounts with full administrator rights; we expected [number]."]
- [An example from the list above that applies to us.]

Any one of these can lead to [a data breach / fraudulent payments from spoofed email / an outage of a business
system / a failed audit].

**What we propose**

- Run Maester every day against our Microsoft 365 environment. It reads our settings; it does not change them.
- Send the results to [the security team / IT operations]. Serious findings are raised as [tickets / alerts].
- Report a short summary to [this group] every [month / quarter].

**What it costs**

- Licence: none. Maester is free and open source.
- Running costs: [about $X per month for the cloud service that runs it / none, it runs on existing infrastructure].
- Staff time: [N days] to set up, then [N hours per week] to review results and fix findings.

**What you will see**

A short [monthly] update: the number of serious findings open, how many were fixed, and anything that needs a
decision from you.

**Decision requested**

Approval to [set up Maester and assign N hours per week of (team)'s time to act on its results] by [date].

  stored secret [where possible]. See [Monitoring with Maester](monitoring/readme.mdx).

- Runs in [our GitHub Actions / Azure DevOps / Azure Automation / an admin workstation], in [region / environment].
- Results (HTML, JSON, optional CSV and Excel) are written to [storage location], with access limited to [group].
  They describe our security configuration and should be classified as [classification].
- User identities can be replaced in every output with `-RedactUserIdentity AllOutputs`. `HtmlOnly` redacts only
  the HTML report, so the JSON, CSV and other machine-readable outputs keep the real identifiers. Redaction is best

  `-ExcludeAffectedObjects` leaves it out.
- Results are sent only where we configure them: [email recipients / a Teams channel / none].

  which tests or what they found. For now each run also sends a legacy usage event, which holds our tenant ID, to
  PostHog. See [What telemetry does Maester send?](faq.md#what-telemetry-does-maester-send). We will turn both off
  with `-DisableTelemetry`. [Yes / No]
- With `-SkipVersionCheck` the module makes no calls to check for a newer version.

- Source code is public and reviewed through pull requests; each test is a readable PowerShell file.
- We will [pin a specific module version](installation.mdx#update-or-pin-a-version), review release notes before

| --- | --- |

| A malicious or faulty module release | Version pinning, review before upgrade, [internal repository] |
| False assurance from passing tests | Maester tests configuration only; it complements, and does not replace, [penetration testing / SIEM / audit] |

Assessment and approval to run Maester [version] with the access above, from [environment], by [date].

## Common questions

**We already have Microsoft Secure Score. Why add this?**
Secure Score is Microsoft's view of recommended settings, and it is worth tracking. Maester adds what Secure Score
does not: tests for your own rules (this group must be in this policy, this app must not have these permissions),
results mapped to the framework you report against, a daily result in your own pipeline with alerts, and the
CIS, CISA, ASD Essential Eight, DISA STIG, EIDSCA and ORCA checks side by side with Maester's own.

**We already have a SIEM, Defender and Sentinel.**
Those watch for activity: sign-ins, alerts, attacks in progress. Maester checks configuration: whether the controls
that prevent those attacks are still in place. A weak setting produces no alert until it is exploited.

**It is free. Who supports it?**
Maester is an open-source project maintained by a community of Microsoft security practitioners, with the source
on [GitHub](https://github.com/maester365/maester). Questions and bugs go to
[GitHub issues](https://github.com/maester365/maester/issues), and the open-source module comes with no support
contract.

If your organisation needs a product with a vendor behind it, [Maester Cloud](https://maester.cloud) runs Maester in
your own Azure subscription, keeps the results over time and alerts you when something changes. It comes with
support for setting it up and running it, and each subscription also funds the open-source project.

Because Maester reads configuration and runs in your own environment, the operational risk of a fault is low: the
worst case is a run that fails or a test that reports the wrong result, which you can turn off.

**Will it change anything in our tenant?**
Not by default: Maester asks only for read permissions. Two optional permissions are off unless you ask for them:
sending mail, and `-Privileged`, which lets one test turn off report obfuscation while it runs and then turn it back
on. The SharePoint tests need a full-control SharePoint permission, although they only read.

**How much work is it?**
A first report takes about fifteen minutes. Scheduled monitoring takes a few hours to set up with one of the
[monitoring guides](monitoring/readme.mdx). The ongoing work is fixing what it finds, which is the point.

**We will get hundreds of failures on the first run.**
That is normal, and it is the reason to start. Fix Critical and High first, record the findings you accept as
exceptions with a reason, and report the trend. See [Getting started](getting-started.mdx#step-3-fix-what-matters-first).
